# Uncertainty, need for closure, seizing and freezing

## Central idea

Uncertainty does not produce one psychological response. It can promote exploration, but it can also increase motivation for a definite answer. Need-for-closure theory describes this pressure for certainty and two associated tendencies: “seizing”, relatively rapid adoption of an available explanation, and “freezing”, maintaining it in the face of later information.

## A motivational construct, not an intelligence label

Kruglanski and Webster define need for closure as a desire for definite knowledge on an issue rather than ambiguity or confusion. They treat it both as an individual-difference dimension and as a situationally evocable state. Time pressure, noise, fatigue and the perceived cost of uncertainty can alter closure motivation.

This matters for CEM because it blocks a moralizing interpretation. Reaching a conclusion quickly does not automatically imply low cognitive capacity; it can be adaptive under time, cost or risk constraints. The epistemic problem arises when premature closure costs more than it saves and when later relevant information is filtered or reinterpreted to preserve the initial conclusion.

## Seizing and freezing are different moments

Seizing describes urgency to attain closure. An early cue, a simple explanation or a source perceived as certain can receive disproportionate weight. Freezing describes permanence: after a conclusion is formed, search may decline and new information can meet resistance.

The distinction is useful for modelling. A mechanism for initial hypothesis selection is not the same as a mechanism for conservative updating after commitment. If CEM makes this layer executable, each stage will need an operational measure, a differential prediction and a rejection criterion.

## Relationship to uncertainty and resources

Need for closure is context-sensitive but should not be equated with stress. Stress, fatigue and time pressure may change processing costs and make rapid responses more attractive, yet these constructs are not identical. Chapter 4 treats stress and executive resources separately.

MOD.02 and MOD.06 retain these ideas in the conceptual map. The current simulator has no numeric “closure” state.

## Why CEM does not treat Track A / Track B as populations

A binary population classification would require direct measurement, validated thresholds and evidence that two discrete classes fit better than continuous, context-dependent variation. CEM does not have such evidence. Historical Track A/Track B language can at most serve as a metaphor for processing modes, not as prevalence estimates or fixed person types.

## What this chapter does not claim

It does not claim that need for closure is pathological, inevitably produces misinformation belief, or can be inferred from political orientation, education or online behavior. It does not assign CEM agents a closure score and does not treat closure as a universal cause of false belief.
